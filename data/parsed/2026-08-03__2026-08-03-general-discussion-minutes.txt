2.  Association of O&C Counties Presentation 
Tim Freeman, President of the Association of O&C Counties, and Doug Robertson, 
Executive Director, presented information regarding the history and purpose of the 
Association, current federal land-management and revenue issues, the value of the 
Association’s advocacy to Josephine County, and the County’s potential return to 
membership. 
Mr. Robertson explained that the Association of O&C Counties was formed in Grants Pass 
in 1925 so the counties containing Oregon and California Railroad revested lands could speak 
with one voice before Congress and the federal land-management agencies. The 
approximately 2.5 million acres of O&C lands had been conveyed to a railroad company to 
support construction of the rail line and were later returned to federal ownership after the 
railroad failed to comply with the conditions of the grant. Because the revested lands could 
not be taxed or developed like private property, the counties organized to seek economic 
benefit from the lands and to participate in decisions affecting their management. 
Mr. Freeman stated that the checkerboard pattern of public and private ownership was never 
intended to create a conventional national forest. The O&C lands were established as timber-
producing lands and are governed by the O&C Act of 1937, which directs that the lands be 
managed under sustained-yield principles while supporting stable communities, recreation, 
watersheds, and other public values. 
The presenters explained that the 1937 O&C Act initially provided the counties with 50 
percent of timber receipts while the federal Treasury recovered the cost of reacquiring the 
lands. That obligation was satisfied in 1952, and the counties received the full 75-percent 
share in 1953. Beginning in 1953, the counties voluntarily reinvested the additional 25 
percent in a plowback fund used for roads, bridges, reforestation, recreation facilities, fire 
suppression, culverts, and other management of the O&C lands. Mr. Freeman estimated that 
the counties’ historical investment through the plowback program would equal approximately 
$2 billion in current dollars.

During the early 1980s, Congress began including language in the Department of the Interior 
appropriations bill directing that the counties’ additional 25-percent share be used first to 
support the Bureau of Land Management’s O&C program. Mr. Freeman reported that the 
Association began working in approximately 2018 to remove that appropriations language. 
The current Interior appropriations measure no longer contains the provision, allowing the 
counties to return to the 75-percent distribution established under the O&C Act. 
The presenters discussed the County’s recent election under the Secure Rural Schools and 
Community Self-Determination Act. Counties had previously expected to make separate 
elections for Forest Service and O&C lands. The federal government later interpreted the 
reauthorization as requiring a single election covering both land categories, which created 
uncertainty for O&C counties whose timber receipts could exceed their SRS allocation. 
Mr. Freeman stated that the Association continued working with the Oregon congressional 
delegation and the Department of the Interior through the election deadline. The Department 
ultimately determined that a county would receive the payment associated with its SRS 
election and, separately, its proportional share of 25 percent of current O&C receipts. 
Josephine County had elected SRS and, based upon the information presented, was expected 
to receive its SRS allocation together with an additional estimated O&C distribution of 
approximately $1.8 million. The presenters stated that Josephine County receives 
approximately 12.08 percent of the total O&C county distribution. 
The estimates were based upon approximately $60 million in anticipated O&C timber 
receipts for the current federal fiscal year. The BLM generated approximately $65 million in 
receipts during the prior year. Mr. Freeman emphasized that the figures remained estimates 
because timber harvest, market conditions, wildfire, litigation, and the timing of federal 
payments could affect the final amount. 
The presenters stated that, before the separate 25-percent distribution was confirmed, an 
election to receive timber receipts was projected to provide Josephine County approximately 
$5.6 million, compared with approximately $4.3 million through SRS. They explained that 
member counties received detailed election estimates and frequent updates as federal 
interpretations changed. Because Josephine County was not a member, the County did not 
receive the same internal analyses before its election was submitted. 
Mr. Freeman stated that the Association’s adopted position is to remove O&C lands from 
future SRS reauthorizations and reconnect the counties directly to the 75-percent timber-
receipt formula. He explained that O&C receipts are generally available as General Fund 
revenue, while portions of Forest Service SRS funding are restricted to specified purposes. 
The current SRS authorization was expected to expire at the end of the federal fiscal year 
unless Congress enacted another extension. 
The presenters described four principal areas of Association activity: legislation, coordination 
with federal land-management agencies, public education and outreach, and litigation. The 
Association monitors and advocates on federal legislation and appropriations affecting O&C

lands; participates in BLM planning and management processes; educates public officials and 
the public regarding the history and economic significance of the lands; and participates in 
litigation involving resource-management plans and individual timber projects. 
The Association works with federal elected officials, the Department of the Interior, national 
and Oregon BLM leadership, and other agencies. Mr. Freeman stated that the unified 
representation of the O&C counties allows the Association to obtain meetings and responses 
that an individual rural county would have difficulty obtaining on its own. The Association 
also partners with the American Forest Resource Council in litigation and has intervened in 
cases to support timber sales and defend the counties’ statutory interests. 
The Association’s budget includes federal lobbying, legal counsel, technical resource 
expertise, accounting, and staff support. The presenters identified a federal lobbying contract 
of approximately $240,000 annually and a legal retainer of approximately $5,000 per month, 
with additional costs for significant cases. The full membership adopts the annual budget, an 
accounting firm handles financial transactions, and policy direction is provided by a board 
composed of one commissioner from each member county. 
Mr. Freeman discussed the current BLM resource-management plan and stated that 
approximately 80 percent of the O&C land base is placed in reserves, with critical-habitat 
designations limiting management on a substantial portion of the remaining acreage. He 
estimated that approximately 11 percent of the land base is presently available for active 
timber management. 
The Association requested a full revision, rather than a limited amendment, of the resource-
management plan. It is participating as a cooperating entity in the current revision process. 
The existing plan identifies an annual sustained-yield level of approximately 205 million 
board feet, which the BLM has not achieved. The direction published for the revision 
discusses returning toward historical harvest levels approaching one billion board feet 
annually. The presenters stated that the O&C lands grow approximately 1.2 billion board feet 
each year and could support a substantially larger and sustainable harvest. 
The Board discussed available milling capacity. Although Josephine County no longer has a 
sawmill, the presenters reported that substantial processing capacity remains in Douglas and 
Lane Counties and described recent private investments in modern mills. They stated that 
additional federal timber availability could support existing mills and encourage continued 
investment, although markets, transportation costs, and the three-year federal timber-sale 
cycle affect when timber is harvested and when receipts are received. 
The presentation addressed the effects of overstocked stands, delayed thinning, and the failure 
to salvage and replant severely burned federal timberlands. Mr. Freeman stated that private 
and public examples demonstrate that burned areas can be safely salvaged and reforested, 
while untreated fire areas may retain hazardous dead trees, reburn, impede firefighting, and 
remain unproductive for decades. The discussion also addressed wildlife mortality during

Mr. Freeman and Mr. Robertson requested that Josephine County consider rejoining the 
Association. Annual dues were estimated at approximately $68,000 and are calculated in 
proportion to each county’s share of O&C receipts. No back dues would be required. The 
Association offered to discuss prorating or phasing the dues because the County’s fiscal year 
had already begun. 
Membership would allow the Board to designate one Commissioner to serve on the 
Association’s board, participate in policy direction, receive current financial and legal 
analyses, and report Association activities back to the full Board. The next Association board 
meeting was expected to be held in September in Polk County, with the annual meeting 
anticipated in December. 
Commissioners requested additional information before making a membership decision, 
including the Association’s budget, legal and lobbying expenditures, and written responses 
to questions provided during the meeting. The Board discussed possible prorated 
participation, the anticipated additional O&C revenue, and the appropriate County budget 
process. The Commissioners expressed appreciation for the presentation and the 
Association’s advocacy but did not make a formal membership or expenditure decision.

Critical Minerals in Oregon
Oregon Department of Geology and Mineral Industries
Securing a resilient supply-chain of domestic critical minerals 
and materials is essential to the economic prosperity and 
national security of the U.S. In the 21st century, the global 
need to decarbonize energy production, manufacturing, and 
transportation is shifting focus from traditional high-value 
resources (e.g., gold) to a wide range of materials. Oregon has 
a rich mining history, dating back to gold discoveries in the 
1850s. Metal extraction has been dominated by gold, silver, 
copper, and nickel. Many critical minerals have only become 
important in the past 50 years, hence they were often 
discarded with mine waste materials in favor of prioritizing 
economic mineral targets.
Oregon’s historic mine records provide a foundation to 
guide mineral exploration in the State, now with a critical 
mineral focus, and to engage with interested parties 
throughout the process: from initial mapping, to green-energy 
education and careers, and mine life-cycle management.
The 2025 Critical Minerals Lists sees the addition of 10 
minerals, compared to 2022: boron, copper, lead, metallurgical 
coal, phosphate, potash, silicon, silver, rhenium, and uranium.
Oregon is home to a unique and varied geology, formed by 
volcanic and tectonic events that have resulted in a wide range 
of critical mineral deposits: at least 40 of the 60 critical minerals 
are indicated within the State in historic records, with others 
anticipated by common mineral associations.
The Department of the Interior (DOI) has published lists of Critical Minerals every three years since 2018. As required by The 
Energy Act of 2020, the U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) has updated the list for 2025. The new list includes 60 minerals, highlighted 
by the elements in the Periodic Table below.

Geologic carbon storage (GCS) is the process of capturing carbon dioxide (CO2) from industrial sources or the atmosphere, 
compressing it into a ﬂuid, and injecting it deep underground into suitable geological formations to be permanently stored.
Most types of GCS system store CO2 as a ﬂuid in the subsurface, and require long-term monitoring to ensure that ﬂuid does 
not leak in the future. However, CO2-rich ﬂuids will react with the minerals in basalt, turning that CO2 into stone over time (mineral 
trapping). This process occurs slowly in nature, and is accelerated with the addition of higher CO2 concentrations in the ﬂuid.
Potential CO2 reservoirs in the CRBG reside in the Grande Ronde 
Basalt, far below the potable water zone in the Columbia Basin. 
The Grande Ronde includes ~100 individual lavas, with a total 
thickness up to 14,760 feet and covering ~65,600 square miles. 
It is estminated that the Grande Ronde may be capable of 
storing 40 billion metric tons (Gt) of CO2.
Grande Ronde lavas consist of fractured, weathered, 
fragmented, and vesicular top and base zones, and denser 
interior zones that often have colonnade joints. Lava tops and 
bottoms can have porosity up to ~40%, whereas unit interior 
porosity is typically 1-3%. Lava tops and bases may be good 
reservoirs for CO2 storage, with lava interiors acting as seals.
The stratigraphic research well will allow characterization 
of rock properties, to determine the suitability of the basalts 
for CO2 mineral storage, at great depth.

Geothermal Energy in Oregon
Oregon Department of Geology and Mineral Industries
Geothermal energy is a crucial potential source of constant, 
24/7 renewable power in the Western U.S. This potential is due 
to the region's active geology, marked by tectonic plate 
boundaries, volcanic activity (such as the Cascade Range), and 
hot spot areas. While California and Nevada currently lead the 
nation in installed geothermal electricity capacity, Oregon is 
one of the few states with existing utility-scale geothermal 
power generation and consistently ranks among the top states 
for geothermal potential. With known resources in the 
Cascade Range and the Basin and Range Province of its central 
and southeastern counties, Oregon is well positioned to 
leverage 
both 
conventional 
(hydrothermal) 
and 
next-generation technologies, such as Enhanced Geothermal 
Systems (EGS), to expand this reliable resource and help the 
entire Western grid transition to a clean-energy future.
OVERVIEW
WHAT IS GEOTHERMAL ENERGY
Deﬁnition: Geothermal is a renewable energy resource that 
taps into the heat beneath the Earth's surface. Drilled wells can 
extract hot ﬂuids (water or steam), which are used to generate 
electricity or provide direct heating. Unlike solar or wind, 
geothermal is not dependent on weather or time of day, 
making it a reliable, constant (baseload) power source for the 
grid.
WHY GEOTHERMAL MATTERS
Reliable Baseload Power: Unlike solar and wind, geothermal 
runs 24/7, providing system stability.
Low Emissions & Clean Energy Goals: Helping Oregon to 
meet its goals of 100% clean electricity generation by 2040.
Small Land Footprint: Requires signiﬁcantly less land per 
megawatt than other renewables.
Economic Impact: Provides local jobs in rural communities 
and stable energy costs.
BARRIERS AND CHALLENGES
High Upfront Costs: Exploration and drilling can be 
time-consuming and expensive.
Resource Risk/Uncertainty: Currently there are large data 
gaps for assessing geothermal resources (heat and ﬂow).
Siting and Permitting: The need for careful siting to avoid 
impacts on environmental areas (e.g., hot springs).
Types: Geothermal energy is used in two main ways: Electricity 
generation uses high-temperature ﬂuids to spin turbines; Direct 
Use and district heating use lower temperature hot ﬂuids to heat 
homes, businesses, and pools, displacing fossil fuels and saving 
money. Oregon is a leader in both applications.
New technologies: Enhanced Geothermal Systems (EGS) and 
Superhot Rock Geothermal could help access areas of hot, dry 
rock where natural ﬂuids are lacking, signiﬁcantly expanding 
Oregon's resource potential (e.g., Newberry Caldera projects).

Oregon holds signiﬁcant geothermal assets associated with 
volcanic features such as the Cascade Range and High Lava 
Plains, as well as systems associated with high heat ﬂow caused 
by crustal extension and thinning in the Basin and Range 
Province of southeastern Oregon.
Oregon hosts utility-scale geothermal power plants, such as 
the Neal Hot Springs facility in Eastern Oregon. Oregon is a 
national example of Direct Use, notably in Klamath Falls, which 
for decades has used geothermal heating for its downtown 
buildings and the Oregon Institute of Technology. Recently, the 
Newberry Geothermal System has become globally signiﬁcant 
as a premier site for Enhanced Geothermal Systems research, 
demonstrating Superhot Rock technology can economically and 
reliably tap extreme heat from deep, dry rock.
Mazama Energy operations in Newberry, OR, where a 
temperature of 331°C has been reached.
The western U.S. holds substantial untapped geothermal energy 
potential, but development is often hindered by inaccessible data. 
Public and private geothermal datasets are scattered across state 
geological surveys, private companies, and universities in various 
formats. This impedes regional analysis, elevates exploration risks, 
and discourages investment, limiting geothermal expansion.
DOGAMI is joining with state surveys in NV, WA, UT, ID, MT, AZ, and 
CA to develop a uniﬁed, publicly accessible, interactive geothermal 
data platform known as WestGIN. The project will streamline data 
access, build collaborations among state surveys, prioritize data 
collection and dissemination, and create regional products from data 
synthesis including geothermal playbook chapters. In doing so, 
DOGAMI will fulﬁll its mission of providing earth science information 
and regulation to make Oregon safe and prosperous.

Geologic Hydrogen in Oregon
Oregon Department of Geology and Mineral Industries
Secure and reliable energy is critical to U.S. national security, 
especially as the demand for clean fuels used in electric power 
generation, manufacturing, and transportation become a 
priority. Factors including population growth, the increasing 
energy needs of data centers, reshoring of manufacturing, and 
the electriﬁcation of transport and other sectors are straining 
our ability to meet energy demands. To address this, the U.S. 
is exploring the potential of clean, low-carbon hydrogen as a 
new, abundant, and domestic energy source. Hydrogen is a 
versatile fuel suitable for transportation, industrial processes,

CURRENT & EMERGING USES OF HYDROGEN
Transportation: used in hydrogen fuel cells to 
power electric vehicles with zero tailpipe emissions
Industrial: a key ingredient in ammonia, for 
fertilizers, cleaning products, and refrigerants
Industrial: replacing coal as both the source of heat 
and the reducing agent in steel making
Aerospace: used as a rocket propellant and 
potentially in aviation fuel for longer-range aircraft
Energy Storage: improving grid stability for energy 
produced by intermittent sources (e.g., solar, wind)

Geologic hydrogen (sometimes referred to as white or gold 
hydrogen) is sourced or released from underground rocks by 
natural processes, and is generated naturally by several 
processes (right).
• Serpentinization: water oxidizes iron-rich minerals in rocks 
(e.g., olivine and pyroxene). Oxygen from the water binds 
with iron in the rock, leaving behind free hydrogen.
• Radiolysis: natural radiation from radioactive elements 
present in rocks, breaks down water molecules (H2O), to 
produce free hydrogen.
• Biological activity: hydrogen produced by microbial life 
during metabolism (metabolic hydrogen or biohydrogen), 
such as through fermentation or nitrogen ﬁxation.
• Thermal cracking of hydrocarbons: organic matter deep 
within crustal basins break down to form hydrogen.
These natural processes can be replicated and engineered to 
increase hydrogen production rates, such as production from 
fossil-fuels using a process known as steam methane 
reforming. This process uses large amounts of energy and 
produces substantial amounts of carbon dioxide as a 
by-product. Hydrogen can also be produced by electrolysis of 
water, which can have a very low environmental impact if using 
100% renewable or nuclear energy sources.

Recent U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) reports[1] suggest 
geologic hydrogen exists in quantities large enough to make a 
signiﬁcant contribution to the U.S. energy portfolio (see 
overleaf). USGS prospectivity models suggest that Oregon has a 
high potential for hydrogen sources, both at the surface and at 
depth (below). Those models provide a useful basis for early 
exploration work in Oregon and can be used as a guide for 
future geologic mapping and rock characterization speciﬁcally 
for geologic hydrogen potential.
One of the primary natural source mechanisms for hydrogen 
production is serpentinization: the hydration of iron–bearing 
minerals, such as olivine and pyroxene, in mantle peridotites 
(e.g., right). Serpentinization is a “redox” (oxidation-reduction) 
reaction, in which the ferrous iron (Fe2+) is oxidized to ferric iron 
(Fe3+), and water (H2O) is reduced to release molecular 
hydrogen (H2). This released hydrogen can be stored in the 
subsurface if there is a seal and reservoir present, it may react 
with subsurface compounds and minerals, or it can migrate 
upward toward Earth’s surface. The USGS maps therefore 
indicate probability of the ingredients for hydrogen, but are not 
a guarantee that hydrogen will be present.
HYDROGEN IN OREGON
NEXT STEPS
In DOGAMI's role as a regulatory and geological survey, we 
intend to facilitate the research and development of geologic 
hydrogen resources in Oregon. The next steps would be to 
collect geophysical data, such as gravity, magnetic, and 
electromagnetic data to characterize the shallow subsurface 
and evaluate the potential for hydrogen sources, reservoirs, 
and seals across the state. Data collected by drilling 
information holes could be used to further evaluate 
subsurface conditions. Notably, exploration is classiﬁed as 
an outright land use in Oregon, and exploration projects 
need only landowner approval to proceed to permitting.
DOGAMI’s research and exploration of geologic hydrogen 
resources will be guided by outreach and engagement with 
communities and Tribal Nations at every step of the process. 
DOGAMI will work with potentially impacted parties to 
understand concerns and perspectives, and inform ongoing 
project planning while openly and directly addressing 
community feedback.
By putting community collaboration at the forefront of the 
research and development process, and incorporating 
industry-established best practices, DOGAMI will fulﬁll its 
mission of providing earth science information and regulation 
to make Oregon safe and prosperous.
